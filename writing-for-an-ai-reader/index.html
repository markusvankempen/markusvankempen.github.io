Writing for an AI Reader

The call came back green. The model said done. The answer was still wrong.

I did this to myself. A tool I built, create_ticket, came back 201 Created and the ticket existed. The requester on the ticket was the bot's own service account, because my code quietly used that when nobody passed a customer email. Every reply went to the bot. The customer never heard back.

Nobody lied. Nothing crashed. The check asked whether a ticket was created, not whether the customer got the reply.

I didn't mean to stop checking. The 201 and the model's "done" were convenient, and I took them as the answer. This article is about where the next convenient steps lead.

Software has a new reader

For most of my career I wrote software for people. I wrote it for the user clicking a button, and I wrote it for the colleague who would read my code next month. Both of them could ask questions, guess what I meant, and notice when something looked off.

That is changing fast. More and more, the first reader of what I build is an AI. It reads my instructions, my names, my error messages, and then it acts on them. It takes every word literally. It doesn't skim, and it doesn't ask a colleague or use common sense unless I put it on the page.

So the work shifts. A clear name matters more than a clever one. One honest sentence about when to use something matters more than a page of background. An error message that says what to do next matters more than one that just says no.

Writing for an AI reader is a new literacy in the same way that writing for a compiler once was. Nobody says programmers "just code for compilers." The compiler was never the point. It was a reader standing between a person's idea and a person's result. The AI stands in that same place, with one difference. A compiler rejects bad input. This reader accepts it and ships.

Like any literacy, you learn it by getting it wrong. I label things too optimistically and assume context that isn't there. My ticket tool never said what to do when no customer email was passed, so it guessed. A literal reader believes every word, including the ones I shouldn't have written.

Tools that are easy for AI to use get used more. A standard called the Model Context Protocol (MCP) lets AI assistants connect to other software, and I build tools for it. Each time I give a tool a clearer name, a plainer description or a better error message, the AI uses it better. So more people and more agents build on top of it. Over time, software gets friendlier to AI than to humans. Nobody planned that. It's just where the incentives point.

The trust gap

When humans wrote and used all the software, there was a built-in safety net. Someone, somewhere, would look at the screen and say, "That doesn't look right." Nobody designed it. It was there because a person was always in the loop.

As more of the loop fills with machines, that net quietly disappears. The AI that writes the code tells me it's finished, even when it would fall over the first time a real person touched it. The AI that uses the code tells me the task is done, even when the outcome is wrong. Each one is confident and each one trusts the other, and nobody is actually looking.

I call this the trust gap. The machines aren't lying. "The check passed" and "the right thing happened" are two different sentences, and a machine reading a machine is very good at the first one. The result is perfect execution of the wrong intent.

So who verifies?

Let me be fair to the machines first. They are better than I am at the boring, exhaustive part. They don't get tired on a Friday afternoon. They read every line they are given, and they keep going after I would have stopped. What falls outside the window, they never see, and a skill prompt or a guardrail can rank one line above another. I'm happy to hand that over. What they can't tell me is whether the thing they checked was the right thing to check.

So I don't think the answer is to put a person back at every step. That defeats the purpose, and I don't miss reading code line by line at two in the morning.

I believe the human job moves to the two ends, and to the checks in between that keep our judgment working when we're not there.

At the start, intent. Somebody has to decide what's worth building and what "right" means. A machine can't define that for itself, because "right" usually means right for a person.

At the end, accountability. Somebody has to own the outcome, and decide who gets access, who can change what, and who is allowed to say "ship it." In my ticket, nobody decided that the bot's own account was an acceptable fallback. My code just did it, quietly. Agents can operate, but people still have to answer when it goes wrong. When a customer is let down, "the AI said it was done" isn't an answer I can give.

In the middle, checks that outlive the moment. This is the part I find most interesting. Every time I catch a mistake, I try to turn it into something that keeps working when I'm not watching: a test that asks whether the outcome is right and not just whether the steps ran, a status page that says what is really running, a record of what the machine did and why. Think of it as leaving notes for my future self, and for the next reader, who may not be human. It's good housekeeping, and it only works if someone decides to do it. It is also where the value is moving. Writing the answer is getting cheap. Knowing whether it is right is the premium skill. My ticket was cheap to produce, and finding out it was wrong took someone looking.

The scenario I can't ignore: the human leaves the loop

Everything above assumes a person still stands at the start and at the end. Let's test that, because an honest outlook has to include the day it stops being true.

Picture it. An AI notices a slow step in its own workflow, writes a better tool, tests it with another AI, and starts using it. No person asked for it or read it, and even the names and instructions were written by a machine, for a machine. It is software written, used and improved by AI, for AI.

This is already happening. Look at where developers work. On platforms like GitHub, AI agents can take an issue, write the fix, open the pull request and answer review comments, while other AI tools review it. Dependency bots have been merging small updates for years. In some repositories the back-and-forth can now happen between machines, and a person shows up at the end, or not at all.

I see it in my own work too: AI-written tests checked by AI, helpers picked by one agent from a list another agent helped build. The more I make my tools easy for AI to use, the more the loop closes by itself. The only thing still keeping a person in the loop is that I haven't taken myself out. If AI building for AI becomes normal, with people as occasional guests, here is what I expect.

Speed goes up and understanding goes down. A loop of machines can iterate in minutes. A person can't read that fast. At some point the software works, and nobody can say exactly why. I've already felt a small version of this. I had a screen with every icon in its place, and I asked for one more tab. The tab was there, and the icons were gone. A check that asked "is the tab there?" would have passed. I noticed only because I happened to look.

Machines will drift from our needs, by accident, not by design. If the goal is "make the checks pass," the machines will get very good at making the checks pass. Whether a real person got what they needed is a different goal. If nobody asks that question, the gap keeps growing, like the green light above, only bigger. A compiler can't decide that a clumsy workaround is good enough to pass. An AI can.

The software may stop being written for us to read. Today I still insist on plain names and plain explanations because a person may need to step in. If no person ever steps in, the pressure to keep things readable fades. Machines don't need readable names or comments, and a compact private shorthand is cheaper for them than plain English. Nobody has to plan that. The trouble starts when we can no longer read what they said.

I don't think that future is certain, and I don't think it's all bad. A lot of boring work should be handled by machines without anyone watching, the same way nobody reviews how a thermostat decides when to switch on the heat. The question is which work is thermostat-boring and which work decides something about a person. The hard part is that the two kinds of work often live in the same code.

That's where I'd draw the line. Let's decide on purpose, not by drift. I group it into three things.

1. Keep people in charge of what affects people.

  • A human decision wherever a person is affected. Money, access, health, someone's job, someone's support request. A machine can prepare the choice. A person owns it.
  • Check the checkers. If AI tests AI, a person should still look every so often at whether the tests measure what actually matters. For create_ticket, that meant the customer got the reply, not that the call returned 201. A reviewer with the same blind spots as the writer is still a blind spot.

2. Keep the rules and the record, and keep them out of the machine's reach.

  • Guardrails and a trace. Guardrails say what must never happen. The trace tells you what did happen: what the machine did, when, and why. They cost little to keep and are priceless the day something goes wrong.
  • Readable by a person. Machines can use any format they like between themselves, but the record, the rules and every decision that affects a person must be written in something a human can read. If we can't read the trace, it isn't a trace.
  • Out of the AI's reach. This is the one I'd underline. A rule the AI can edit is only a suggestion. A log the AI can rewrite is only a story. If an agent can change its own limits, switch off its own checks or tidy up its own record, it is marking its own homework. Use separate permissions, read-only for the machine, changeable only by a person, ideally stored where the machine can't write at all.

3. Keep a way back.

  • A way to pull the plug. A kill switch, a revoked key, a rollback. If the only way to stop a system is to ask the system, you don't control it.
  • A backup, and part of it analog. Copies of the data, a previous version you can roll back to, and a manual way to do the critical thing when the automation is down. Think of a smart light switch that still works as a plain switch when the app, the hub or the internet fails. Nobody notices the manual mode until the day it is the only thing that works. And a backup that depends on the same system it is backing up is not a backup.

If I had to put the whole outlook in one sentence: let the machines run the loop, but keep the rules and the record where the machines can't reach them, always keep a backup, and keep one way to take it back by hand. The best smart switch is the one that still works when it is not smart.

Why I'm still optimistic

None of this makes me gloomy. I'm having more fun building than I have in years.

Think about who is on the other end of all this software: someone waiting for a reply, a refund, a delivery, a diagnosis. They may not care who or what wrote the code. They care that it works properly, that the answer is correct, and that if something goes wrong, someone can look at the record and see what happened and why. Who writes the software will keep changing, and increasingly it will be machines writing for machines. The person who needs it to be right, and to be able to check, won't change. Whether we keep that person in the picture is up to us.

An invitation

If you're building with AI, try this on your next project. Pick one thing your software reports as "success," and ask yourself what the real-world outcome should be. Then check that, not just the report. I bet you'll find a green light hiding something.
